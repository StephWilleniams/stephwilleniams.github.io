Stephen Williams

I build mathematical models to predict how partnerships between hosts and microbes form, and how they break down under stress, especially climate-driven warming. My work combines low-Reynolds-number fluid dynamics, mathematical modeling and data-driven inference, developed in close collaboration with experimental labs. My main systems are the bobtail squid and its bioluminescent symbiont Vibrio fischeri, and the sea anemone Exaiptasia diaphana, a laboratory model for coral bleaching.

I am part of NSF INSITE, the Institute for Symbiotic Interactions, Training, and Education in the Face of a Changing Climate, working with Profs. Erica Rutter and Shilpa Khatri. I did my PhD in physics at the University of Warwick with Prof. Marco Polin.

Research

Symbioses can fail at any scale: in the flows a host uses to recruit its microbes, in how microbes adapt to stress, or in host populations. I build mechanistic models at each scale, then use sensitivity and identifiability analysis to show experimental collaborators which measurements matter most.

Fluid dynamics

Symbiont recruitment in the bobtail squid

Newly hatched bobtail squid draw in their bacterial partners using breathing-driven flows. With the Heath-Heckman lab (Michigan State), I built a regularized-Stokeslet model of these flows inside the squid. It shows that the shallow, rapid breathing of heat-stressed squid can halve the time bacteria spend near the light organ.

J. R. Soc. Interface (2026) · Code
Inference

Models of microbes and hosts under stress

With the Nishiguchi lab, I analyzed the identifiability of a V. fischeri biofilm model, using a genetic algorithm to design sampling schedules: 11 optimized time points beat 51 evenly spaced ones. With the Sogin lab, I study heat stress in Exaiptasia, where shifts in rare bacterial taxa precede the host's own decline.

Bull. Math. Biol. (2026) · Nat. Commun. (in press)
Active matter

Active–passive mixtures in confinement

In my PhD, I combined swimming microalgae and passive colloids in microfluidic channels, showing that confinement alone drives colloids to pile up at the walls, which we used to de-mix the two experimentally. I am now generalizing this to other microswimmers and to the living boundaries of hosts.

Interactive: Stokes flow from point forces

At the scale of bacteria and the cilia that move them, viscosity dominates and flows are built from point forces called Stokeslets. Regularized Stokeslets are the method behind my squid flow models. Click to add one, drag it around, and watch tracers follow the flow.
