At the Mathematics of Data, Dynamics, and Life Sciences meeting at UC Irvine in January 2025, I presented a poster on modeling symbiosis establishment in juvenile *Euprymna scolopes*.

**Authors:** Stephen Williams, Kyra Ruiz, Elizabeth Heath-Heckman, Erica Rutter, Shilpa Khatri

## Abstract

Symbioses underpin the survival of many organisms, but rapid environmental change may expose hidden vulnerabilities in these partnerships. The bobtail squid and its bacterial partner *Vibrio fischeri* are a model system for studying how flows created by the ciliated structures of the light organ recruit bacteria. We simulate bacterial motion in these flows and examine how climate-driven changes could affect colonization.

## Summary

Juvenile squid acquire their symbionts from seawater by breathing, and cilia on the light organ's appendages capture the bacteria. We modeled these flows with the method of regularized Stokeslets [9, 10], varying breathing strength, breathing frequency, ciliary beating and squid size, and measured how long bacteria stay near the light organ.

A Sobol sensitivity analysis [8, 11] across 81,920 simulations showed that breathing frequency has the strongest individual effect. Faster, harder breathing, as seen under thermal stress, shortens how long bacteria stay near the light organ, suggesting heat stress could hinder colonization.

This work became our paper on [thermal stress and symbiotic fluid dynamics](/updates/thermal-stress-disrupts-symbiotic-fluid-dynamics-in-bobtail-squid/).
